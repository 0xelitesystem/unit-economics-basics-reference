# Contribution margin

Contribution margin is the revenue from one unit minus the variable costs of producing and delivering that unit. It is what each sale contributes toward covering the fixed costs of the business and, beyond that, toward profit. Because it strips out only the costs that rise with each unit, it isolates whether the act of making one more sale puts money in or takes money out.

## Variable versus fixed costs

The distinction at the heart of contribution margin is between variable and fixed costs. Variable costs change with each unit: materials, payment processing, shipping, the direct cost of serving one more customer. Fixed costs do not change with volume: rent, salaries, software, the overhead that exists whether you sell one unit or thousands. Contribution margin subtracts only the variable costs, because those are the costs directly caused by the unit itself.

## How to read it

A positive contribution margin means each unit brings in more than it costs to produce and deliver, so every sale helps pay down fixed costs and then adds profit. A negative contribution margin means each sale loses money on its own, before fixed costs even enter, which is a serious warning: selling more makes things worse. The size of the contribution margin also tells you how many units you must sell to cover fixed costs and reach breakeven.

## Why it comes first

Contribution margin is the foundation of unit economics because it determines whether scale can ever help. If each unit contributes something positive, growing volume eventually covers fixed costs and turns profitable. If each unit contributes nothing or less, no amount of volume rescues the business; it just loses faster. This is why analysts start here: a healthy contribution margin is the precondition for everything else, and a negative one signals a problem that growth cannot solve.
