# Why it decides scaling

Unit economics decide whether a business can scale because scaling multiplies whatever is true of a single unit. If each unit is profitable, growth compounds profit; if each unit loses money, growth compounds losses. This is why sound unit economics are the precondition for scaling, and why growth alone is not evidence that a business works.

## Scale multiplies the unit

Growing a business means doing the same unit many more times. Whatever the economics of one unit are, scale magnifies them. A business that earns a healthy margin on each customer turns more customers into more profit, and overhead gets spread thinner per unit, improving results further. A business that loses money on each customer turns more customers into bigger losses, no matter how fast it grows. Scale is an amplifier, and it amplifies whatever sign the unit economics carry.

## The trap of growth without economics

A common and dangerous pattern is rapid growth built on broken unit economics, where a company spends heavily to acquire customers who are worth less than they cost. The top-line growth looks impressive and can attract attention and funding, but each new customer deepens the loss. Such a business is running faster toward running out of money. Growth masks the problem until the spending must stop, at which point the unprofitable core is exposed. Unit economics is the check that catches this early.

## The discipline it imposes

Looking at unit economics forces the right question before scaling: does each unit pay for itself with margin to spare. If the answer is yes, investing in growth is investing in profit. If the answer is no, the priority is fixing the unit, raising contribution margin, lowering CAC, or increasing LTV, before pouring money into expansion. This discipline is why investors and operators study unit economics closely: they reveal whether a business is ready to scale or whether scaling would only accelerate its problems. This is an explanation of the framework, not financial advice.
