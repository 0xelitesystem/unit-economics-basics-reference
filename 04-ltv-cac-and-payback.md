# LTV:CAC and payback

The LTV to CAC ratio compares the lifetime value of a customer to the cost of acquiring them, and the payback period measures how long it takes to recoup that acquisition cost. The ratio tells you whether customers are worth more than they cost, and the payback period tells you how quickly the money comes back. Both translate raw CAC and LTV into signals about the health and sustainability of growth.

## The LTV:CAC ratio

The ratio divides lifetime value by acquisition cost. A ratio of one means a customer is worth exactly what they cost, leaving nothing for fixed costs or profit. A widely cited rule of thumb holds that a ratio around three to one is healthy: customers are worth about three times what they cost to acquire, giving room for overhead and profit. A very low ratio signals that acquisition is too expensive relative to value, while an extremely high ratio can hint that the business is underinvesting in growth.

## The payback period

The payback period is how long it takes for the contribution from a customer to repay the cost of acquiring them. If it costs a certain amount to win a customer who then contributes a steady margin each month, the payback period is the number of months until that margin adds up to the acquisition cost. A shorter payback period means cash returns faster and can be reinvested sooner, which matters enormously for a business funding its own growth. Long paybacks tie up cash and raise risk if customers leave early.

## Why both matter

The ratio and the payback period answer different questions. LTV:CAC asks whether customers are ultimately worth more than they cost, a question of total profitability. Payback asks how fast you get your money back, a question of cash flow and risk. A business can have a strong long-term ratio but a dangerously long payback that strains its cash, or a quick payback but a thin lifetime ratio. Reading both together gives a fuller view than either alone, which is why both appear in any serious unit-economics analysis.
